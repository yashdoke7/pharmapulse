PHARMAPULSE - WHAT IS ON EACH SCREEN, PART BY PART
==================================================

Read this if you are presenting and you did not build it.

You do not need to understand the maths. You need to be able to point at
any panel on any screen and say what it is and why it is there. That is
what this file gives you. Every panel on every screen is listed below.

Read PART 0 first. It is six terms, and once you have them the rest of the
document is just naming boxes on a screen.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 0 - THE SIX WORDS YOU NEED
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Learn these six. Everything else you can read off the screen.

1. FORECAST
   Our guess at how much of a medicine will sell in the coming weeks. We
   do not give one number - we give a range, because a single number
   pretends to a certainty nobody has.

2. ORDER QUANTITY
   How many units to actually buy this week. This is the product. The
   forecast is only the input to it.

3. LEAD TIME
   How long the supplier takes to deliver after you place an order.
   Here it is 4 days by default.

4. REVIEW PERIOD
   How often the buyer sits down and places orders. Here, every 7 days.

5. PROTECTION INTERVAL
   Lead time PLUS review period. 4 + 7 = 11 days.
   This is the one that catches people out. If you order today, the stock
   has to last until the NEXT order arrives - not just until this one
   does. So the order must cover 11 days, not 4. Sizing against 4 was an
   actual bug we had, and it caused constant stockouts.

6. MASE
   The score we use for forecast accuracy. Lower is better.
   Below 1.0 = better than the dumb baseline. Above 1.0 = worse than it.
   Ours is 0.907. The baseline is 1.117.

Two more that only come up if someone asks:

   COST OF BEING WRONG IN TWO DIRECTIONS
   Order too little and you lose the sale and maybe the customer. Order
   too much and your cash sits on a shelf and the medicine can expire.
   Both cost money. The system balances the two.

   PROVENANCE
   For every number on screen, we track where it came from - measured
   from the real sales file, or entered by the user as a setting. It is
   labelled on screen. Nothing is invented.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 1 - THE SHAPE OF THE WHOLE THING
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Seven screens across the top bar, left to right:

   DECISIONS   what needs my attention today
   ORDER       what do I actually buy
   FORECAST    what do we think will sell
   WHY         why is it that number, and can I trust it
   REPLAY      what would this have been worth last year
   EVIDENCE    prove the accuracy claims
   SETTINGS    my shop's numbers - lead time, costs, stock

They are in that order on purpose. It is the order a real buyer's
questions arrive in. You can present the whole thing by going left to
right and never going back.

Underneath the screens there are four parts to the system. If someone
asks "what are the components", this is the answer:

   1. DATA        reads the sales file, checks it, cleans it, builds
                  daily / weekly / monthly views of it
   2. FORECAST    classifies each medicine, runs several models on it,
                  combines them, checks its own confidence
   3. DECISION    turns a forecast into an order quantity, flags risks,
                  tracks the shelf, and can replay history
   4. INTERFACE   the seven screens, plus the API that feeds them

Data flows one way only: 1 to 2 to 3 to 4. Nothing later reaches back.

One more thing worth being able to say: the heavy work happens OFFLINE, in
a batch that runs separately and saves its results. When you click around
the screens, no model is being run - the screens are only reading saved
results. That is why everything is instant.


=========================================================================
SCREEN 1 - DECISIONS
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "What needs my decision today?"

This is the landing screen. A buyer has ten minutes on a Monday morning.
This screen is built for those ten minutes.

THE PARTS ON IT
---------------

(a) THE HEADLINE, top left
    "Four products need a decision, Rs 1,236 at risk."
    A sentence, not a chart. It is a live count over all eight medicines.
    Say: "It does not open on a graph. It opens on a sentence."

(b) THE THREE COUNTERS, top right
    ORDER NOW      - how many products need buying, and how many units
    CAPITAL STUCK  - how many have too much stock sitting idle, and how
                     many days of cover that represents
    HEALTHY        - how many are fine (e.g. 4 out of 8)
    Together they account for all eight products.

(c) THE EXCEPTION LIST, the numbered rows
    One row per product that needs attention. Each row has:
      - a rank number (01, 02, 03)
      - the product code and a coloured badge, STOCKOUT or OVERSTOCK
      - a percentage: how likely that problem is
      - a plain sentence naming the problem
      - a line of explanation ("10.4 days of cover, the order has to
        survive 11 days - a 4-day lead time plus a 7-day gap")
      - the rupee amount at risk, on the right
      - a link that takes you straight to the order that fixes it

    THE POINT TO MAKE HERE. The list is sorted by MONEY, not by
    probability. Look at the rows: one is 99% certain and it is ranked
    LAST because it is only worth Rs 163. One is 34% likely and ranked
    ABOVE it because it is worth Rs 276.
    Say: "A 30% chance on your biggest seller matters more than a 90%
    chance on something that sells twice a month."

(d) SHELF POSITION, the table at the bottom
    All eight products with their current stock, days of cover, reorder
    point, when they run out, and a suggested quantity. This is the "show
    me everything" view for a buyer who does not trust the shortlist.

IF SOMEONE ASKS
    "Where does the percentage come from?"  From the forecast. We forecast
    a range, so we can read off how likely it is that demand exceeds what
    is on the shelf.
    "Where does the rupee figure come from?" For a shortage, it is the
    profit you lose on the sales you cannot make. For overstock, it is the
    cost of having cash tied up in stock you do not need.


=========================================================================
SCREEN 2 - ORDER
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "What do I actually buy?"

This is the most important screen in the whole project. Spend the most
time here. Everything else exists to make this screen possible.

THE PARTS ON IT
---------------

(a) THE PRODUCT TABS, across the top
    Eight medicines. Click one to switch. Each shows the common name
    (Paracetamol, Ibuprofen, Aspirin...) with its official drug code
    underneath.

(b) THE SLIDER PANEL, left, titled "How often are you willing to run out?"
    This is the heart of it. The slider is the ONLY thing a buyer has to
    have an opinion about. Drag it left = accept running out more often,
    hold less stock, cheaper. Drag it right = almost never run out, hold
    more stock, more expensive.

    DRAG IT SLOWLY IN THE DEMO. Four things move together:
      SERVICE LEVEL   the % of the time you will not run out
      STOCKOUT RISK   the mirror of it
      COST THIS CYCLE the total expected cost at this setting
      ORDER (top right) the quantity, in units and in packs

    And say this: nothing is being loaded while you drag. No spinner, no
    network request. The whole cost curve was sent once, and the browser
    is doing the rest.

(c) THE COST CURVE, the graph underneath the slider
    Expected cost at every possible service level. It is U-shaped.
    Left side high  = you run out too often, you lose sales.
    Right side high = you hold too much, you pay to store it.
    The marked point is the bottom of the U - the cheapest place to be.
    Say: "The recommendation is not a rule of thumb. It is the bottom of
    that curve."

(d) THE PLUS/MINUS ONE PACK PANEL, below the curve
    Three prices side by side: one pack fewer, the recommendation, one
    pack more. E.g. Rs 39.02 / Rs 38.58 / Rs 39.07.
    Say: "This is what makes it usable. The buyer can see that being one
    pack off in either direction costs about fifty paise. They can argue
    with the number instead of just obeying it."

(e) THE ACCEPT BUTTON
    Black button, "ACCEPT 240 UNITS". Clicking it actually records the
    order and moves the shelf. It is not a mock-up.

(f) POSITION, right column, top
    On hand, reorder point, days of cover, when it runs out, target level,
    and the recommendation. The "on hand" number is not stored anywhere -
    it is the starting stock plus every movement since.

(g) WHAT THE POSITION IS MADE OF, right column
    The movement trail. Opening stock, then every receipt and sale, adding
    up to the number on the shelf. This is why the position is auditable -
    you can see what it is made of.

(h) LEAD-TIME DEMAND, right column
    Five bars: p5, p25, p50, p75, p95. Read it as: "there is a 5% chance
    demand over the next 11 days is below 287, a 50% chance it is below
    399, a 95% chance it is below 546."
    This is the forecast RANGE, over the whole 11-day protection interval,
    and it is what the order is read from.

(i) WHERE EACH INPUT CAME FROM, right column, bottom
    The provenance panel. Every input labelled MEASURED (from the real
    sales file) or YOUR SETTING (entered by the user). Point at it if
    anyone asks whether data was invented.

IF SOMEONE ASKS
    "How does it decide the quantity?"
    There is a formula. If running out is three times as expensive as
    over-ordering, you order enough to cover demand three times out of
    four. That ratio comes from your own costs. The formula is a
    hundred years old and it is exact - we did not invent it, we shipped
    it. It is called the newsvendor formula.

    "Why packs?"
    Medicines come in boxes. You cannot order 23.4 units. It rounds up
    when running out is the more expensive mistake.


=========================================================================
SCREEN 3 - FORECAST
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "What do we think will sell, and do I believe it?"

THE PARTS ON IT
---------------

(a) DAILY / WEEKLY / MONTHLY toggle, top right
    Three different clocks, three different questions:
      Daily   - will I run out before the next delivery?
      Weekly  - what do I order on Tuesday?
      Monthly - how much cash do I need, and when?
    Switch between them in the demo. It is a good ten seconds.

(b) THE FAN CHART, the big graph
    Blue line, left half   = what actually sold. Real history.
    Dashed vertical line   = today. Everything right of it is a forecast.
    Black line, right half = our forecast.
    Three grey bands       = the 50%, 80% and 90% ranges.
    The bands widen as you look further ahead, which is correct - you know
    less about eight weeks from now than about next week.
    Say: "It gives a range, not a single number, because a single number
    pretends to a certainty nobody has."

(c) THE HORIZON SLIDER, above the chart on the right
    How many periods ahead to forecast. 8 by default.

(d) THE MEMBERS TABLE, bottom left, "THE MEMBERS BEHIND THE MEDIAN"
    Five rows, five different forecasting models, and their predictions
    for each of the next several periods. The last row, in green, is the
    Ensemble - which is the MIDDLE value of the five.
    Say: "Five models, each with a different idea of how demand works. We
    do not pick one. We take the middle value. Screen 6 shows why that is
    the more interesting result."

(e) SERIES PROFILE, bottom right
    Facts about this medicine: its demand class, two statistics called ADI
    and CV-squared, its daily average, how often it sells nothing, and its
    peak month.
    The demand class ("smooth", "intermittent", ...) decides WHICH models
    this medicine gets routed to. It is recalculated every night from the
    data - nobody configures it by hand. One product, the sedatives, sells
    nothing on 68% of days, so it goes to models built for that.

IF SOMEONE ASKS
    "Why five models instead of the best one?"
    Because we tested picking the best one and it scored worse. See
    screen 6. Different models make different mistakes; taking the middle
    value cancels them out.


=========================================================================
SCREEN 4 - WHY
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "Why is it that number, and are you sure?"

Two panels. They answer two different questions. Cover both.

LEFT PANEL - ATTRIBUTION, IN UNITS
----------------------------------
    A headline like "Antihistamines is down 20 units next month", then
    the reasons, each as a bar with a number:
        Seasonality   -23.7 units    "coming off the pollen season"
        Trend          +4.0 units    "underlying level"
        Total change  -19.8 units

    Say: "A normal machine learning tool would show you a chart of which
    variables mattered. That explains the MODEL. A buyer needs an
    explanation of the QUANTITY. So the answer is in units, and the parts
    add up to the whole. There is a test that checks they add up, because
    an explanation that does not add up to the number it explains is worse
    than no explanation at all."

RIGHT PANEL - ARE OUR CONFIDENCE INTERVALS HONEST?
--------------------------------------------------
    This is the panel most projects never build. Take your time.

    What it is: we went back and checked our own predictions. Every time
    we said "we are 80% confident demand will be in this range", did the
    real answer actually land in that range 80% of the time?

    The answer: no. It landed there 92.2% of the time.

    That means our range was TOO WIDE. Which sounds like the safe
    direction and is not - a range that is too wide pushes the order
    quantity up, so the buyer buys more than they need and pays to store
    it. We corrected it to 82.0%.

    The graph:
        Dashed diagonal = perfect. Closer to it is better.
        Red line   = before correction. Bowed above the diagonal.
        Black line = after correction. Sits on the diagonal.
        "n = 256" = how many checks that is based on. Stated on purpose.

    Say: "We measured whether to believe ourselves, we did not like the
    answer, so we fixed it."

    Worth adding: our own design document had predicted the opposite -
    that our ranges would be too narrow. The measurement contradicted the
    document, and we corrected the document.

BOTTOM STRIP - A STATED LIMITATION
    A paragraph at the bottom of several screens. It says we forecast
    SALES, not DEMAND. See the closing section of this file.


=========================================================================
SCREEN 5 - REPLAY
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "What would this actually have been worth?"

THE PARTS ON IT
---------------

(a) THE WINDOW TABS, top right
    Three real quarters of 2019/2018 history to replay.

(b) THE REPLAY PANEL with START REPLAY
    Click it and it plays the real history one day per tick. Real sales,
    real dates. The date advances and the shelf moves as sales happen and
    deliveries arrive.
    Say: "Nothing here is simulated except the shelf and the ordering
    policy. The sales are the real 2019 sales."
    Let it run 15 seconds, then stop it. Do not let it run the whole
    quarter on camera.

(c) WHAT IT WAS WORTH - four cards. This is the business case.

    Card 1 - PHARMAPULSE. Our total cost for the window.

    Cards 2-4 - the three baselines, each with a chip saying whether we are
    cheaper or they are. Green means we win, amber means they do. THE AMBER
    ONES ARE ON THE SCREEN ON PURPOSE.

      Min/max on the mean          the "no system at all" case - a spreadsheet.
                                   We beat it in all three windows (+6%, +49%,
                                   +61%). Do NOT lead with this: anyone who
                                   works in inventory discounts it instantly,
                                   because every ERP carries safety stock.

      (s, S) safety stock          what real inventory software actually does.
                                   We are LEVEL with it - we win one window
                                   (+23%) and lose two by a couple of percent
                                   (-2.9%, -1.8%).

      Our forecast, normal sizing  THE ONE TO LEAD WITH. It gets our forecast,
                                   our protection interval and our service
                                   level. It differs in exactly one thing: it
                                   sizes with a normal approximation instead of
                                   reading the quantile off the calibrated
                                   distribution. So forecast quality is held
                                   constant and the gap is the distribution and
                                   nothing else. We win all three (+17.9%,
                                   +8.1%, +0.4%).

    Every policy sizes off the SAME trailing window of real sales, so the
    comparison is about the decision rule and nothing else.

    WHAT TO SAY
    "Beating a spreadsheet is easy, so we built the two baselines that are
    hard. The one on the right gets our forecast and our service level and
    differs in exactly one thing - how it turns that into a number. We beat it
    on every window, so the distribution is earning its place. Against a real
    ERP policy we are level, and that is on the screen in amber rather than
    left out."

    IF ASKED WHY WE DO NOT BEAT THE ERP
    "Every policy here sizes off a trailing window, so none of them can see a
    seasonal turn coming - on the first of January the last 180 days are
    autumn. Anticipating it is exactly what our forecast layer is for, and the
    replay does not exercise it yet: that needs a forecast produced at each
    review point rather than one vintage. It is written down as the next piece
    of work."

    AND SAY THIS PART TOO
    Where we ARE cheaper, look at the breakdown: our holding cost is HIGHER.
    The saving is entirely fewer lost sales, and there is a test asserting that
    relationship so the claim cannot quietly invert.

(d) The paragraph under the cards
    States that both policies were given the identical days, identical
    costs, identical lead time, identical review cadence and identical
    protection interval. The only difference is that the old policy sizes
    against the AVERAGE and we size against the range.

IF SOMEONE ASKS
    "Is min/max a fair comparison?"
    Yes, and we gave it every advantage - the same protection interval as
    ours. It is the standard policy, it is what a real shop uses, and an
    earlier version of this comparison was accidentally unfair to it (it
    produced an 88% saving). We found that and corrected it down to 70%.


=========================================================================
SCREEN 6 - EVIDENCE
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "Prove it."

This is the screen that makes a faculty member believe the other five.
Do not skip it and do not rush it.

Open with this line: "Every number on this screen was written by a script
that runs from a clean copy of the project. None of it is typed by a
human. The script writes a file, and this screen only reads that file."

THE PARTS ON IT
---------------

(a) THE FOUR FIGURES, across the top
    ENSEMBLE MASE     0.907   - our accuracy score
    BENCHMARK TO BEAT 1.117   - the baseline's score
    INTERVAL COVERAGE 82.0%   - from screen 4
    CACHE HIT RATE    0%      - "no model runs per request", i.e. the
                                screens only read saved results

    Say: "0.907 against 1.117 is 18.8% better than the benchmark. And the
    benchmark is not a weak one - it is 'repeat what happened this week
    last year', and on pharmacy data that is genuinely hard to beat,
    because pharmacy demand really is seasonal."

(b) THE HIGHLIGHTED BOX - "THE RESULT WORTH LEADING WITH"
    Three numbers:
        Pick the best model per product   0.968
        Combine them (take the middle)    0.907
        Perfect hindsight                 0.843

    THIS IS THE BEST THING IN THE PROJECT. Say it clearly:
    "We built the obvious approach - work out which model is best for each
    medicine and use that one - and we measured it LOSING. Selection
    scores 0.968. Combining scores 0.907."

    Why: with only about 300 weekly data points per medicine, 'which model
    was best last time' is mostly luck. So selection chases luck. Whereas
    different models make different mistakes, and taking the middle value
    cancels them out.

    "Perfect hindsight" at 0.843 is what you would score if you could see
    the future and always pick right. It is a ceiling, not a model. We
    include it so you know how much room is left.

(c) MODEL LEADERBOARD, bottom left
    Every model we tried, as a bar chart, best at top. Green is ours.
    Amber is the benchmark. It shows we beat all eleven individual models.

(d) PER SERIES - INCLUDING WHERE WE LOSE, bottom right
    All eight medicines, the benchmark's score, our score, and the best
    single model for that one.

    SAY THIS PART OUT LOUD, DO NOT LET THEM FIND IT:
    "We beat the benchmark on all eight. So that comparison is not the
    interesting one. The honest column is the absolute one - a score above
    1.000 means worse than simply repeating last week, and two of our
    eight are. Antihistamines at 1.646 is our worst. Its peak is in May,
    it is sharp, and its timing moves year to year - and we only have six
    years, so six observations of it. We are not going to pretend
    otherwise."

    Say why this matters: a team that only reports its wins gets
    discounted, and experienced judges do that quickly.

(e) PROVENANCE, the footer strip
    Snapshot hash (a fingerprint of the exact data file), generation
    timestamp, model version, audit chain valid, ensemble members, how
    long the backtest took, and how many model-fold combinations were run.
    Say: "If the data file changes, that fingerprint changes, and every
    claim on this screen is tied to it."

IF SOMEONE ASKS
    "How do I know it is not typed in?"
    Run  python scripts/day1_benchmark.py  from a fresh copy. 45 seconds.
    It rewrites the file this screen reads. The same script runs
    automatically on every push to GitHub.

    "What is MASE and why not percentage error?"
    MASE compares us to the dumb baseline. Below 1 is better than it,
    above 1 is worse. We cannot use percentage error because one of our
    medicines sells zero on 68% of days, and you cannot compute a
    percentage error against zero.

SCREEN 7 - SETTINGS
=========================================================================

The question it answers:  "These are my shop's numbers, not yours."

THE PARTS ON IT
---------------
    Lead time (days), review period (days), holding cost rate, expiry risk
    rate, and per-product stock on hand, unit margin, unit cost and pack
    size.

THE DEMO TO DO HERE
    Change LEAD TIME from 4 days to 9 days. Save. Go back to ORDER.
    The recommended quantity jumps.
    Say: "The supplier got slower, so the order has to cover a longer
    window. Nothing was retrained. The forecast did not change. Only the
    decision changed."

WHY THIS SCREEN EXISTS AT ALL - THE MOST IMPORTANT IDEA IN THE PROJECT
    The data file we were given has dates and units sold. That is all. It
    has NO stock levels, NO prices, NO costs, NO lead times. Those columns
    do not exist.

    So we did not invent them. We split every value in the system into
    three lanes with different rights:

    LANE 1 - MEASURED. The sales file and things worked out from it.
             Can train a model. Can explain. Can back an accuracy claim.

    LANE 2 - YOUR SETTING. Lead time, costs, stock, pack size - the things
             on this screen. Can appear in an explanation as a named
             input. NEVER trains a model. NEVER backs an accuracy claim.

    LANE 3 - MADE UP. Not allowed anywhere. The code physically refuses to
             load a made-up data file - it throws an error - and there is
             a test that checks it does.

    Why it matters, in one sentence: if you invent a price column and
    train on it, your model has learned from a random number generator,
    and then the "Why" screen shows that noise to a pharmacist as if it
    were a real commercial reason. That is worse than having no
    explanation.

    Every accuracy number in this project comes from the sales file alone.

THIS IS THE ANSWER TO THE HARDEST QUESTION YOU WILL BE ASKED
    Q: "Where did the cost and stock data come from? You made it up."
    A: "Yes, and that is exactly why it is quarantined. Those are lane 2 -
       user settings. They are entered on the Settings screen, labelled as
       settings everywhere they appear, they never train a model and they
       never back an accuracy claim. In a real deployment they come from
       the pharmacy's own system."


=========================================================================
THE CLOSING - THE LIMITATION TO STATE BEFORE ANYONE ASKS
=========================================================================

Say this at the end. Volunteering it is the strongest thing in the
presentation.

    "We forecast SALES, not DEMAND.

    When a shop runs out, the sales record shows zero. But zero sales is
    not the same as zero demand - customers came and were turned away, and
    nothing recorded that. So our data understates demand, and it
    understates it worst on exactly the products that matter most.

    Left uncorrected it feeds itself: a stockout lowers recorded sales,
    which lowers the forecast, which lowers the order, which causes
    another stockout.

    What we do about it: we flag days where sales hit a suspicious
    ceiling. What we cannot do: verify the correction, because this
    dataset has no stock column to check against. It is written down in
    our model card, and it is the first thing a real deployment would fix,
    by connecting to the pharmacy's own inventory system."


=========================================================================
IF YOU ONLY REMEMBER FIVE THINGS
=========================================================================

1. It does not stop at a forecast. It produces a purchase order, in packs.
   Almost every tool in this market stops at the chart.

2. The list is ranked by MONEY, not by probability. A 30% chance on your
   biggest seller beats a 90% chance on something that barely sells.

3. We checked our own confidence and found it wrong (too wide, 92% instead
   of 80%), and corrected it.

4. We built the obvious approach - pick the best model per product - and
   measured it LOSING to just combining all of them.

5. The data has no costs or stock, so we never invented them. They are
   settings, they are labelled as settings, and they never touch a model.
